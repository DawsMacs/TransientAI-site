Research / Recursive meta-adaptive learning

Recursive meta-adaptive learning

"Recursive meta-adaptive learning" (RMAL) is our name for a direction: learning that continues while the system is used, changes the system's internals as it goes, adapts how it learns and not only what, and in the end revises that adapting process itself. It is an ambition we are working toward, not something the prototype has reached, and not a settled definition.

An illustration of the direction

The goal stays. The way it learns changes.

A robot has one task: deliver a light across a changing course. A correction helps it once. Learning how to adapt helps it face what comes next.

Read the sequence
  1. Attempt. The robot follows a familiar route and meets a closed gate.
  2. Correct. It updates its knowledge and takes the open passage.
  3. Reconsider. A second gate changes. Remembering the last route is no longer enough.
  4. Test. It changes its learning strategy: compare possible routes before committing to a move.
  5. Revise. It uses the outcome of those tests to change how it chooses the next test. When another obstacle appears, it probes the uncertain passage instead of testing every route again. Feedback is now revising the process of adaptation itself.
  6. Complete. It delivers the light, carrying a better way to adapt into the next challenge. This is the direction we hope to pursue.

Why not just train a model?

The usual pipeline is to pretrain on a very large corpus, post-train, freeze the weights and serve them. Training is an event that happens once, somewhere else, for everyone. That has costs we would like to avoid for a system meant to run on a personal machine:

  • Facts are learned expensively. One study reports that transformers store roughly 2 bits of knowledge per parameter, and only after seeing a fact on the order of a thousand times (Allen-Zhu and Li). Writing a line to a store takes one step and no gradients.
  • Updating weights can forget. In one continual-learning test, fine-tuning on new facts reduced performance on old ones sharply, while updating only a few memory slots reduced it far less (sparse memory finetuning). We have not reproduced these figures.
  • A frozen model cannot learn from you, privately and cheaply, after it ships.

Three levels of adaptation

These are the levels we use to say how far the prototype goes.

  1. Adapting the knowledge. New facts, new words, corrections. baby does this in real time: one new lesson costs about half a millisecond.
  2. Adapting how much and when it learns. Steps per lesson, when to sleep, what to ask, how far to trust a match. Our first versions had these as constants chosen by hand; the move is to make each a quantity the system measures from its own experience. So far: the reliability bar (set from the noise it measures in its own lessons) and how boldly it guesses (set from its own track record). Several others remain constants.
  3. Adapting the learning mechanism itself. For each kind of thing it meets, try different ways of learning it and keep whichever generalizes, judged on facts it held back. This is the level that most distinguishes the work, and the one where the prototype is both most interesting and most limited.

What "recursive" adds

Levels 1 to 3 are adaptation that we set up: the system tunes and chooses, but the procedure that does the tuning and choosing is ours. A fixed procedure that adjusts its own settings is still programmed adaptation. The recursive step is the one beyond level 3: the system revising the process that does the adapting, by means we did not hand it in advance. That step is what would separate genuine adaptation from a very good learning algorithm, and it is the part the prototype does not do. Everything below describes levels 1 to 3 only.

What the prototype does at level 3

Given groups of lessons and no instructions, baby chooses per group among remembering, fitting a rule, selecting, searching for a small program, or training a small network. Some outcomes from the synthetic curriculum:

  • Sums, differences and doubling became rules; "which is bigger" became a selection; multiplication and "how far apart" became programs it built (first × second, first apart second) and that stayed right beyond the numbers it was taught; capitals and colors stayed memorized because nothing could be worked out.
  • Given a heap of 792 unlabeled lessons, it found 9 units itself (the teacher had named 13; some followed one rule and were merged), with a scorecard identical to the teacher-organized version.
  • In the invention battery it worked out all 16 hidden numeric kinds nobody wrote a mechanism for, and 7 of 9 harder ones by reusing constants and programs learned from earlier kinds as vocabulary. Taught in the wrong order, the harder ones failed more often (8 of 72), so the gain came from learning and not luck. It explained 0 of 120 unexplainable kinds (random codes, primes) and answered 0 wrongly.
  • It asks. Where two explanations both fit, it names the number that would tell them apart. With digit sum taught on 0 to 15, one such question took it from 88.9% to 100% of unseen questions; random questions needed three.

Caveat

The limit is large and we want it visible. The mechanisms, the expression language and the thresholds are ours. The system builds and selects inside a language we gave it. Calling that "invention" means expressions and vocabulary, not a new kind of learner. Mechanisms of a different kind, not a formula, are not yet started.

Learning that stays light

Learning should be light enough that nearly anyone can do it, without a data center. Three things measurably help:

  • A surprise gate. A lesson the unit can already work out is neither learned from nor stored.
  • Refit, do not re-decide. A lesson consistent with a settled rule just refits it exactly; the expensive cross-validation of all mechanisms only runs when something structural changed.
  • Exact where possible. Fitting a rule is solved, not iterated: 392× fewer operations than the 800-pass gradient descent it replaced, for the same rule.

In the scale test, learning one more fact stayed at roughly half a millisecond from 20 to 600 units. More in efficiency

Learning in real time

One advantage we hope to keep is that a user's correction can change behavior immediately, without a training run. A concrete case: when baby guesses beyond what it was taught and a teacher says it was wrong, it stops guessing in that region right away. Before this was built, after one correction 303 of the 305 other wrong guesses stayed wrong and 2 became wrong facts; after it, none stayed wrong and none became wrong facts (they were all refused). At first it only stopped; it did not learn the new regime from one point, so the right guesses nearby were refused too. It now rebuilds the law from the numbers the correcting lessons name, and in our test all 157 of the wrong guesses became right guesses after one correction. That comes with a price: it is careful about overruling the old law, so some coverage is lost when the teacher is sometimes wrong.

Terms

Recursive meta-adaptive learning is the project's own term, used as a direction and not an exact definition. It may shift as the work does. It is the earlier working name "adaptive learning" with the recursive step named. The second row is how we compare systems.

Recursive meta-adaptive learningA direction, not a measured property: learning that is continuous and from interaction, with internals that change (adaptive); a learning process that itself adapts (meta); and an adapting process the system revises itself (recursive). The prototype reaches the first two in small, and not the third.

Next steps on this thread

  • Run on a factoid corpus as well as word problems (word problems have had a first look) to see which of this survives contact with messy text.
  • Win back the coverage the repair guard costs, without letting wrong facts return.
  • A teacher who repeats a wrong answer: since 2026-10-25 the baby holds an answer that contradicts a settled rule until it is repeated, or until one law explains several held answers together (a rule that really changed). Believed mistakes fell from 20, 14 and 10 to 5, 7 and 6 when 10% of the facts are told wrongly, and from 41, 25 and 28 to 10, 10 and 18 at 20% (a plain memorizer: 32 and 59 on one stream), with no damage to things it was never wrongly told. These are simulated teachers on tidy streams. What is left is almost all in kinds with no law to check an answer against, which one source cannot settle; a second, independent source is the untried idea.
  • Cheaper adapting: learning from a told answer costs 36 mJ now, about 600 times answering without learning. The remaining savings are the risky ones, and each has to be checked against a stream with a wrong teacher before it is accepted.
  • Adapt more of the remaining constants from measurement.
  • A unit that starts tiny and grows capacity only when it saturates.
