Research / Efficiency

Efficiency, measured in joules

Parameter count says how big a model is, not what it costs to use or to teach. The measure we care about is capability per unit of computation, as the machine itself measures it. So every result here comes with a cost.

How we count

The code has a ledger that counts every multiply-add and every 4-byte memory access. It prices them with the widely cited 45 nm figures from Horowitz (ISSCC 2014): about 3.7 pJ for a 32-bit float multiply, 0.9 pJ for an add, and 640 pJ for a 32-bit read from DRAM. A modern chip spends less in absolute terms, so treat the joules as an estimate and trust the ratios.

What counting showed early on, with the first small neural network: answering one question cost about 14,000 multiply-adds, and about 91% of the estimated energy was moving memory, not arithmetic. Even a 63 KB network is a data-movement problem. A 7-billion-parameter model does roughly 500,000× more arithmetic per token and cannot keep its weights in cache at all.

The same counting showed that implementation is a real lever: writing a dot product as eight independent sums instead of one let the compiler vectorize it with no unsafe code and no special flags, taking a question from 32 µs to 13 µs with identical answers.

Answering: fewer operations, same answers

Using the mathematics of the computation, the prototype now finds the right kind of lesson with a precomputed table of how telling each word's shape is for each unit, instead of comparing the question with every lesson it was ever taught. Remembered lessons are found through an index of their content words, and networks are no longer trained where cheaper mechanisms already work.

8,977 operations / question
One small network
7,104 (memorizes; cannot work out a new sum)
What baby does
57, about 159× fewer, ~85× less estimated energy, same answers

Caveat

Honesty about this figure. These are counted operations, not a meter. The "slow way" is our own first design, which compared against everything, so a saving of this size comes from an inefficient baseline. The reach check that guards extrapolation is not yet counted in the 57. "One small network" is our earlier single network, not a clever alternative.

Learning: light enough for one laptop core

The claim worth testing is that learning should not need a data center. On one CPU core of an ordinary laptop, with no GPU:

WhatCost
Teach 792 lessons in 13 units, from nothing0.34 s (was 3.8 s), about 12 MB peak
A lesson of a kind it already understands0.19 ms, not stored
A new way of saying something it knows0.4 ms
A new kind of lesson, no unit named1 ms (a unit opens at the sixth)
Fit a rule: exact solve vs 800-pass gradient descent392× fewer operations, about 60× faster
Scale test: 25,776 lessons, 600 units, 10,000-word vocabulary2.65 s, 56 MB peak
Learn one more fact, from 20 to 600 units0.4 to 0.6 ms at every size
Reorganize (merge and split) at 600 units1.35 s

Teaching dropped from 3.8 s to 0.34 s mostly because networks stopped being trained for plain tables, and the scale test only stayed flat after two fixes: a per-lesson table that was rebuilt from scratch each time (about 40 ms at 200 units) became incremental, and routing became sparse.

Adapting from being told. Learning from every told answer first cost 1.8 J a question. After two changes that left the replies byte-identical it is 36 mJ, so the 787-question adaptive stream costs 28.5 J (65 J with the teaching), still about 600 times the cost of answering without learning. A plain transformer fine-tuned on each told answer is cheaper per answer (1.6 to 34 mJ) and does not learn from being told. One change that looked identical on every clean stream and the whole test battery was dropped because it changed two answers under a wrong teacher (see how we judge results).

What we have not done yet

  • Real joules. The CPU's own energy counter is now read during the entry exam (package energy above idle, teaching and answering separately). Measured joules exist only on Linux with hardware that exposes the counter; elsewhere the report says it is unavailable. The figures above are still time, memory and counted operations; the exam's readings are from one machine and not yet compared with another system.
  • The controlled comparison. The entry exam, static and adaptive, now exists as one: the same lessons, judge and meter for the baby and a matched plain attention transformer (three seeds, 60 units). The transformer fits what it was taught, then states 1,500 to 2,800 wrong facts and answers 43 to 100% of the traps (the baby: 0 and 0); teaching costs it 11 to 24 times the baby's joules and answering 2.2 to 2.7 times. It is one small untuned model on a busy machine. Not done: a larger or tuned transformer, and a pretrained model on real text, which needs a rented GPU, which is planned. It is the experiment that would say whether any of this matters beyond tidy tasks.
  • Real text. The numbers come from a world of small numbers and a handful of phrasings. Nothing here says how cost grows on millions of real sentences.

The full derivations and the mistakes made along the way are in the repository's experiment log.
